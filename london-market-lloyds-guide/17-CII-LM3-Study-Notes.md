## LO1 – Framework within which business is conducted (≈5 questions)

### 1.1 The framework

| Topic | Key notes |
|---|---|
| **Domestic vs global markets** | Domestic markets serve local, standard risks under local regulation; global hubs (London, Bermuda, Zurich, Singapore, New York E&S) take large, complex, international risks needing large capacity and specialist expertise |
| **Why a client chooses London** | Capacity, specialist expertise, security and ratings, global licences, broker access, flexibility of wordings, claims reputation; domestic may be cheaper, simpler, local law/language, local claims service, admitted status required by local regulation |
| **Sources of capital** | Lloyd's: corporate members (insurance groups, investors), Names (individuals, now mostly via limited liability vehicles), third-party capital via SPAs and London Bridge (ILS); company market: shareholders' equity of insurers and reinsurers; alternative capital: ILS funds, cat bonds, sidecars |
| **PRA** | Authorises, regulates and supervises insurers for safety and soundness; Solvency UK; internal model approval; Fundamental Rules |
| **FCA** | Authorises and supervises conduct of all insurance firms and intermediaries; Principles for Businesses; ICOBS, PROD, Consumer Duty (retail), SYSC, CASS |
| **SM&CR** | Senior Managers (approved, Statements of Responsibilities, Duty of Responsibility), Certification Regime, Conduct Rules |
| **UK Corporate Governance Code** | Applies to premium-listed companies on a "comply or explain" basis: board leadership, division of responsibilities, composition, audit, risk and internal control, remuneration. The 2024 Code added a board declaration on the effectiveness of material internal controls (from 2026 reporting periods) **[likely]** |
| **Lloyd's principles for doing business** | Lloyd's sets principles for managing agents (e.g., in areas such as underwriting, claims, delegated authority, governance, culture, capital, reserving, conduct, operational resilience) and assesses managing agents' maturity against them, scaling oversight to risk **[likely]** – check the current list in the study text |
| **Solvency II / Solvency UK** | Pillar 1 capital (SCR, MCR, technical provisions, own funds); Pillar 2 governance (system of governance, ORSA, key functions: risk, compliance, actuarial, internal audit); Pillar 3 reporting (SFCR public, RSR private, QRTs) |

### 1.2 Duties of the intermediary when creating a contract
- Types: retail, wholesale/placing, producing, reinsurance brokers, MGAs/coverholders.
- Regulation: FCA authorisation, ICOBS, SYSC conflicts, CASS 5 client money, professional indemnity cover, SM&CR.
- **Role in underwriting**: understand client needs; **make the fair presentation** on behalf of the client (broker's knowledge can be attributed to the insured); choose suitable, secure insurers; negotiate terms; ensure the wording matches what was agreed; achieve **contract certainty**; manage conflicts.

## LO2 – Role, purpose and implications of policy wording (≈16 questions)

### 2.1 Identity of the contracting parties

| Topic | Key notes |
|---|---|
| Why identity matters | Insurable interest; who can claim; who owes the duty of fair presentation; sanctions screening; who receives payment; avoids disputes after mergers and name changes |
| **Joint policy** | Parties with the **same (joint) interest**, e.g., joint owners. Historically, wrongdoing by one could affect all |
| **Composite policy** | One document covering **several parties with different interests** (e.g., owner, lender, contractors). Treated as separate contracts – one party's non-disclosure or fraud generally does **not** prejudice the others **[certain]** |
| **Access by others** | **Contracts (Rights of Third Parties) Act 1999** (unless excluded); **Third Parties (Rights against Insurers) Act 2010** – third party can claim directly against the insurer of an insolvent insured; assignment of policy proceeds; **loss payee** clauses; noted interests; co-insured endorsements |

### 2.2 Importance of policy wording
- **Contract certainty**: agreed terms before inception, documentation promptly after. If not achieved: disputes, delayed claims, litigation, regulatory criticism, reputational damage, uncertainty for reinsurers.
- **Consumer policies** need specific protections: CIDRA 2012 (reasonable care not to misrepresent), Consumer Rights Act 2015 (fairness, transparency), FCA ICOBS (pre-contract information such as the Insurance Product Information Document, cancellation rights – usually 14 days for general insurance), Consumer Duty (good outcomes, fair value).

### 2.3 How policy wordings are constructed

| Topic | Key notes |
|---|---|
| **Clear drafting** | Plain, consistent defined terms; clear insuring clause, exclusions, conditions; avoid conflicting clauses |
| **Ambiguity** | Courts may resolve ambiguity **against the party who drafted** the term (*contra proferentem*); more relevant to insurer-drafted standard wordings; less weight where wording was negotiated between sophisticated parties |
| **Who drafted** | Insurer wording (contra proferentem risk for insurer); broker manuscript wording (drafted for the client – ambiguity may be construed against the insured); market standard clauses (LMA/IUA/Institute clauses – interpreted consistently by courts, widely understood) |
| **Regulation and non-domestic wordings** | Local law may require specific clauses, language, filed forms (e.g., US admitted business), service of suit or choice of law/jurisdiction clauses; using an English wording abroad may conflict with mandatory local law; consumer rules restrict terms |
| **MRC – advantages** | Standard structure; supports contract certainty; one document for all insurers; easier processing and data capture; supports electronic placing |
| **MRC – disadvantages** | Can become long; incorporation by reference to wordings may cause gaps; still often unstructured (PDF); follower conditions add complexity |
| **MRC preparation requirements** | Complete all sections; avoid "to be advised" items at inception; state the full wording or reference precise clause numbers; record subjectivities and deadlines; state written and signed line basis; tax and regulatory information |

### 2.4 Viewing the complete contract wording

| Topic | Key notes |
|---|---|
| **Wording libraries** | Central approved repository of wordings and clauses with version control: consistency, faster drafting, fewer errors, easier comparison and analytics (an AI use case – see [Module 09](09-Advanced-AI-in-London-Market.md)) |
| **Side letter** | A separate document varying or adding to the main contract (e.g., special premium terms, profit commission, cancellation rights). Risk: other insurers, reinsurers or auditors may not see it → contract uncertainty, disputes, possible misrepresentation. Should be avoided or fully disclosed and incorporated |
| **Rules of interpretation** | Words given their natural and ordinary meaning in context; read the contract as a whole; commercial common sense where wording is ambiguous; **specific overrides general**; **written/typed overrides printed**; **ejusdem generis** (general words after a list are limited to the same type); **noscitur a sociis** (a word is known by its context); contra proferentem as a last resort |
| **Influences on wording** | Wording specialists (expert drafting, consistency); insurer-drafted wordings (protect insurer, may be inflexible); specific client requirements (manuscript wordings tailored to the client, often broker-drafted) |

## LO3 – Business planning and capital setting (≈18 questions)

### 3.1 Business planning process

| Topic | Key notes |
|---|---|
| **What a business plan is** | Planned premium by class, territory and channel; expected loss ratios; expenses; reinsurance; capital; strategy and risk appetite. Usually **annual**, with mid-year reviews; Lloyd's syndicates submit a **Syndicate Business Forecast (SBF)** each year for the next year of account |
| **Advantages** | Clear direction; aligns underwriting with capital and appetite; basis for monitoring and accountability; supports regulator and Lloyd's approval; supports reinsurance buying |
| **Disadvantages** | Time and cost; can become out of date quickly (market cycle, catastrophes); may encourage "chasing premium" to hit targets; rigid if not reviewed |
| **Regulatory guidance** | PRA expects business planning within Solvency UK governance and ORSA; Lloyd's issues annual **business planning guidance** (e.g., market loss ratio expectations, growth constraints, minimum standards) **[likely]** |

### 3.2 Monitoring and reporting on the plan

| Topic | Key notes |
|---|---|
| **SBF data provided to Lloyd's** | Premium by class (Lloyd's risk codes) and territory; gross and net loss ratios; expenses and acquisition costs; reinsurance programme; rate change assumptions; new and exiting classes; capital and RDS information **[likely]** |
| **Internal reporting** | Actual vs plan by class; rate change and rate adequacy; loss ratios (attritional/large/cat); exposure vs appetite; reserve development; expenses. Early action when off plan |
| **Why Lloyd's reviews plans** | Protect the Central Fund and the market's rating; ensure profitability and sustainable growth; challenge unrealistic assumptions; maintain market-wide discipline |
| **Lloyd's actions on issues** | Request more information; approve with conditions; restrict growth or **reduce capacity**; require remediation plans; increase capital loading; in serious cases stop writing a class or replace management **[likely]** |
| **Required reports** | Regular returns to Lloyd's – e.g., quarterly monitoring returns, performance management data returns, Lloyd's capital returns, RDS returns, Statement of Actuarial Opinion on reserves, Solvency UK returns **[likely]** – learn the names used in the current study text |

### 3.3 How capital setting supports writing business

| Topic | Key notes |
|---|---|
| **Risk appetite** | The amount and type of risk the insurer is willing to take: **insurance risk** (underwriting, reserving, catastrophe), **credit risk** (reinsurers, brokers, coverholders failing to pay), **operational risk** (people, process, systems, external events, including cyber and DA failures), plus market and liquidity risk |
| **Effect on capital** | More or riskier business, larger lines, higher cat exposure, weaker reinsurance, poor controls → higher SCR/ECA → more capital needed. Capital limits how much business can be written |
| **Lloyd's capital** | uSCR from syndicate model, **ECA = uSCR × 135%**; members provide **Funds at Lloyd's**; Central Fund protects the market **[certain]**. See [Module 07 §A4](07-Advanced-Capital-Finance-Regulation.md) |
| **Enterprise Risk Management (ERM)** | Framework to identify, assess, manage, monitor and report all risks across the organisation: risk appetite statements, risk register, three lines of defence (business, risk/compliance, internal audit), ORSA, stress and scenario testing, risk culture |

### 3.4 The technical account and financial statements

| Statement | What it shows | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Balance sheet** | Assets, liabilities and equity at a point in time | Financial position, solvency |
| **Income statement (profit and loss)** | Revenue, expenses and profit over a period. For insurers (UK GAAP) split into a **technical account** (underwriting: earned premiums, claims incurred, operating expenses, allocated investment return) and a **non-technical account** (other income and costs, tax) **[certain]** | Performance |
| **Cash flow statement** | Cash in and out from operating, investing and financing activities | Liquidity |
| **Management accounts** | Internal, more frequent, detailed by class and team | Decision-making and monitoring against plan |
| **Why produce accounts** | Legal requirement; shareholders, members, regulators, rating agencies, Lloyd's | Accountability and transparency |

| Ratio / term | Formula / meaning |
|---|---|
| **Assets / liabilities** | Owned resources / obligations (claims reserves, unearned premiums are main insurer liabilities) |
| **Profit** | Income minus expenses |
| **Return on capital employed (ROCE)** | Profit before interest and tax ÷ capital employed × 100 |
| **Liquidity** | Ability to pay short-term obligations; e.g., current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities |
| **Gearing** | Debt ÷ equity (or debt ÷ capital employed); higher gearing = more financial risk |
| **Combined ratio** | (Claims incurred + expenses) ÷ earned premium. Below 100% = underwriting profit. **Example:** earned premium £100m, claims £62m, expenses £30m → 92% |

## LO4 – Pricing of risk (≈12 questions)

### 4.1 Basic statistics

| Concept | Meaning / example |
|---|---|
| **Frequency** | How often losses occur (e.g., 50 claims per 1,000 policies = 5%) |
| **Severity** | Average size of loss (e.g., £8,000) |
| **Mean** | Sum ÷ count. Losses 2, 3, 3, 5, 12 (£k) → mean 5 |
| **Median** | Middle value when ordered → 3. Less affected by large outliers |
| **Mode** | Most frequent value → 3 |
| **When to use** | Mean for pricing totals; median when large losses distort; mode to find the most common outcome |
| **Return period** | Average time between events of a given size: 1-in-100 years = **1% annual probability**; 1-in-200 = 0.5% |
| **Probability over several years** | P(at least one in n years) = 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ. For a 1-in-100 event over 10 years: 1 − 0.99¹⁰ ≈ **9.6%** |

### 4.2 Constructing an insurance rate

**Premium terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Written premium** | Premium for contracts incepting in the period (booked) |
| **Signed premium** | Premium processed/signed through the bureau (Lloyd's term) |
| **Gross premium** | Before deduction of brokerage (or before reinsurance, depending on context) |
| **Net premium** | After brokerage/acquisition cost (or after reinsurance) |
| **Earned premium** | Portion relating to the expired part of the policy period |
| **Unearned premium** | Portion for the unexpired period – held as unearned premium reserve |

**Claims terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Paid claims | Already paid |
| Outstanding claims | Reserves on known claims |
| Incurred | Paid + outstanding |
| **IBNR** | Incurred but not reported |
| **IBNER** | Incurred but not enough reported (known claims likely to increase) |

**Building the rate – step by step**

1. **Risk premium** = frequency × average severity. Example: 2% × £5,000 = **£100** per policy.
2. Adjust for **underwriting features**: positive (good risk management, sprinklers, strong claims history) reduce the rate; negative (hazardous occupation, poor housekeeping, high cat exposure) increase it.
3. Adjust for **claims experience** of the insured (experience rating) and trends (inflation).
4. Add **loadings**: expenses, acquisition costs (brokerage), reinsurance cost, cost of capital, profit margin.
   - Office premium = risk premium ÷ (1 − loadings %). With 35% loadings: £100 ÷ 0.65 = **£153.85**.

**Burning cost method** (used where the insured has a credible loss history, common in reinsurance and large commercial risks)

| Year | Losses (developed and trended, £k) | Exposure – e.g., turnover (£m) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 400 | 80 |
| 2 | 250 | 85 |
| 3 | 600 | 90 |
| 4 | 350 | 95 |
| 5 | 400 | 100 |
| **Total** | **2,000** | **450** |

Burning cost = 2,000 ÷ 450 = £4.44k per £1m turnover. Next year turnover £110m → expected losses ≈ £489k. Add loadings to get the premium. Use when history is relevant, credible and adjusted for development, inflation and changes in exposure; avoid when data is thin or the risk has changed.

**Claims triangulation** – shows how claims for each underwriting/accident year develop over time.

| Year | 12 months | 24 months | 36 months |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2023 | 100 | 150 | 165 |
| 2024 | 120 | 180 | ? |
| 2025 | 110 | ? | ? |

Development factors: 12→24 = (150 + 180) ÷ (100 + 120) = 1.50; 24→36 = 165 ÷ 150 = 1.10. Projected 2025 ultimate ≈ 110 × 1.50 × 1.10 = **181.5**. Pricing impact: using only reported claims (110) would understate the true cost; the rate must reflect **ultimate** losses, including IBNR.

### 4.3 Realistic Disaster Scenarios (RDS) and catastrophe models

| Topic | Key notes |
|---|---|
| **RDS** | A defined "what if" event (e.g., a major hurricane hitting a specific city) used to measure a syndicate's gross and net loss from one event across all classes; tests aggregation, reinsurance and capital |
| **Lloyd's RDS framework** | Lloyd's prescribes **compulsory** scenarios for all syndicates (examples include US windstorm, US earthquake, European windstorm, Japanese earthquake, terrorism, marine and aviation collision, cyber and liability scenarios), plus **specific** scenarios for some, and **syndicate-defined** scenarios for exposures not captured by the standard set **[likely]** – learn the current list from the study text |
| **Catastrophe models** | Software (e.g., Moody's RMS, Verisk) simulating thousands of events: hazard, vulnerability, exposure and financial modules. Outputs: average annual loss (AAL), exceedance probability (EP) curves, PML at return periods (e.g., 1-in-200) |
| **Purpose and value** | Pricing, accumulation control, reinsurance purchase, capital setting, regulatory and Lloyd's reporting |
| **Deterministic vs probabilistic** | **Deterministic**: one specified event and its loss (RDS, stress tests) – easy to understand, no probability attached. **Probabilistic**: many simulated events with probabilities – used for pricing, capital and EP curves |
| **Limitations** | Model uncertainty, data quality (geocoding, values), non-modelled perils (e.g., wildfire, flood in some regions), secondary uncertainty; underwriters must apply judgement |

## LO5 – Methods of writing business and distribution (≈24 questions – highest weight)

### 5.1 Types of placement

| Type | Key notes |
|---|---|
| **Subscription market** | Lead sets terms, followers take shares; MRC; written vs signed lines; several liability; GUA for changes; claims agreement parties. See [Module 04 §4](04-Intermediate-Placement-and-Underwriting.md) |
| **Fronting** | A licensed insurer (fronting insurer) issues the policy and reinsures most or all of the risk to another insurer/reinsurer (e.g., Lloyd's syndicate, captive). **Why**: local licensing/admitted requirements, rating requirements, captive programmes, global programmes needing local policies. **Advantages**: access to markets; compliance with local law; local policy and claims service. **Disadvantages**: fronting fee cost; **credit risk** for the fronting insurer if the reinsurer fails; regulatory scrutiny of pure fronting; less control |
| **Layering** | Large limits split into a **primary** layer and **excess** layers (e.g., $25m primary; $50m xs $25m; $100m xs $75m). **Advantages**: more capacity; insurers choose risk level that suits appetite; better price for each layer. **Disadvantages**: complexity; risk of gaps or inconsistent wordings ("follow form" issues); multiple parties in claims; drop-down disputes when lower layers are exhausted or insolvent |

### 5.2 Methods of distribution

| Method | Key notes |
|---|---|
| **Local branch and overseas offices** | London insurers and Lloyd's platforms abroad (e.g., Lloyd's Asia in Singapore, Lloyd's China, Lloyd's Dubai, Lloyd's Europe) write business close to clients via local underwriters or service companies; local licences, language and claims service |
| **Affinity programmes** | Insurance sold to members/customers of an organisation (bank, retailer, association, employer) under its brand – typically travel, gadget, pet, warranty, A&H. Often via a coverholder |
| **Master policy** | One policy issued to an organisation covering many individuals/entities, with **certificates** to each member (e.g., group travel, contractors' schemes) |
| **Open market, DA, facilities** | See LO5.3 |

### 5.3 Types of delegated underwriting

| Type | What it is | Why used |
|---|---|---|
| **Binding authority** | Agreement under which an insurer delegates authority to a **coverholder** to accept risks and issue documents within agreed limits | Access small/high-volume and local business efficiently |
| **Lineslip** | A facility (usually arranged by a broker) where a **lineslip leader** (and possibly a second lead) accepts declared risks on behalf of all participating insurers | Speed and efficiency for a broker's flow of similar risks while keeping London underwriters in control |
| **Consortium** | A group of insurers agreeing to write a class together, with a **consortium leader** underwriting on behalf of all members | Access to expertise or distribution; scale; share infrastructure costs |

### 5.4 Stakeholders' roles and responsibilities

| Stakeholder | Responsibilities |
|---|---|
| **Insurer (managing agent)** | Due diligence, approval, setting authority, monitoring, audit, claims oversight, final responsibility for the risk and regulatory compliance |
| **Coverholder** | Underwrite within authority, issue documents, collect premium, report bordereaux, comply with law and BAA, treat customers fairly, handle claims if authorised |
| **Broker placing the binder** | Places the BAA in London; may handle premium and documentation; manages conflicts if also the coverholder |
| **MGA** | A type of coverholder/agent with underwriting expertise, often across several carriers; may also be a broker – conflict risk |
| **Consortium members** | Agree consortium terms; accept leader's decisions for their share; monitor leader; share costs |
| **Lineslip participants** | Leader agrees declarations; followers bound by leader's decisions within the lineslip; monitor results; broker administers declarations |
| **Conflicts of interest** | Coverholder acting for both insured (as broker) and insurer (binding); commission based on volume not profit; group-owned service companies; must be identified and managed (SYSC 10) |
| **Policy documentation and contract certainty under DA** | Normally the **coverholder** issues policy documents/certificates within authority, using approved wordings; the managing agent remains responsible for ensuring documentation is correct and timely |

### 5.5 Advantages and disadvantages

| Method | For insurers | For coverholders / brokers / members |
|---|---|---|
| **Binding authority** | + Access to local, small risks at low fixed cost; + local expertise. − Loss of direct control; − data delay and quality; − conduct and reputational risk; − higher acquisition cost | Coverholder: + local control, speed, commission/profit share; − compliance burden, audits, reporting costs. Broker: + income and client service; − conflicts |
| **Lineslip** | + Efficient access to a broker's flow; + followers save underwriting cost. − Followers rely on leader; − adverse selection risk | Broker: + speed, certainty of capacity, client service. Leader: + influence and fees. Followers: − less control |
| **Consortium** | + Access to expertise/business not otherwise available; + shared costs. − Dependence on leader's skill; − reputational link to leader | Leader: + scale, fee income. Members: + diversification; − limited control, concentration risk |

### 5.6 Management and controls

| Topic | Key notes |
|---|---|
| **Underwriting controls** | Underwriting authorities, line guides, referral rules, peer review, pricing guidelines, aggregate limits, exclusions lists, business plan limits, audit trail |
| **Conduct risk** | Risk that firm's behaviour causes poor customer outcomes or harms market integrity (e.g., mis-selling, unfair claims handling) |
| **Product risk** | Risk that a product does not meet the target market's needs or fair value (PROD, Consumer Duty for retail) |
| **Selection and approval** | Before joining a lineslip/consortium or granting a BAA: due diligence on leader/coverholder (experience, financials, systems, controls, conduct, sanctions, key staff), business plan fit, terms review, Lloyd's approvals and registration |
| **PRA, FCA and Lloyd's roles** | PRA: prudential risk of delegated business, outsourcing and operational resilience. FCA: conduct and product governance of the distribution chain; coverholders are FCA-authorised in the UK. Lloyd's: coverholder approval, standards, registration, oversight of managing agents' DA controls |
| **Levels of authority** | Full binding authority; limited (e.g., certain classes/limits); **prior submit** (insurer approval before binding); claims authority levels: none (all referred), limited settlement authority, full authority with **loss fund**; **TPA/DCA** used when coverholder lacks claims expertise or for independence and volume |
| **Information provided** | Risk, premium and claims **bordereaux**; financial statements; complaints data; breaches; audit reports; MI on performance vs plan |
| **Continuous monitoring** | Bordereaux validation; premium vs GPI limit; loss ratio trends; data quality; aged debt; complaints; breaches; referrals; site visits |
| **Audit** | Independent check (often by specialist auditors, sometimes coordinated across insurers) of **coverholders, DCAs/TPAs**: compliance with BAA, underwriting files, premium handling and client money, claims handling, documentation, IT and data security, financial crime and sanctions, complaints and conduct, business continuity; findings tracked to closure |

## Practice MCQs

1. A composite policy covering an owner and a lender means: a) fraud by the owner voids cover for the lender automatically b) each party's interest is treated as a separate contract c) only the owner can claim d) the lender must disclose the owner's claims
2. Under the Third Parties (Rights against Insurers) Act 2010, a third party may: a) cancel the policy b) claim directly against the insurer of an insolvent insured c) change the wording d) avoid the contract
3. Lloyd's ECA is: a) SCR × 1.0 b) uSCR × 1.35 c) MCR × 1.35 d) premium × 35%
4. Earned premium £200m, claims incurred £130m, expenses £60m. Combined ratio: a) 65% b) 95% c) 105% d) 30%
5. A 1-in-250 year event has an annual probability of: a) 0.25% b) 0.4% c) 2.5% d) 4%
6. Frequency 4%, average severity £2,500, loadings 30%. Office premium: a) £100 b) £130 c) £142.86 d) £70
7. A deterministic catastrophe approach: a) uses thousands of simulated events b) measures loss from one specified event c) produces an EP curve d) needs no exposure data
8. Which arrangement lets a lead underwriter accept declared risks for all participants under a broker's facility? a) consortium b) lineslip c) binding authority d) fronting
9. The main credit risk in fronting falls on: a) the insured b) the fronting insurer c) the broker d) the regulator
10. A side letter is risky mainly because: a) it is illegal b) other parties may not see it, harming contract certainty c) it must be filed with the FCA d) it overrides English law

**Answers:** 1-b, 2-b, 3-b, 4-b, 5-b (1/250), 6-c (100 ÷ 0.7), 7-b, 8-b, 9-b, 10-b.
